L1793: considered sustainable (FMSY) for all stocks. For herring and blue whiting, it also exceeds the precau-
L1794: tionary reference point (Fpa). This suggests that while current stock levels are relatively stable, pres-
L1795: sure on the stocks remains high, and the absence of enforceable limits continues to undermine sus-

L10264: Indicator definition and purpose
L10265: 
L10266: C_01 — Connectivity Index (Migratory species)
L10267: 
L10268: C_01 assesses whether corridor-scale coordination is an ecologically appropriate spatial response for
L10269: priority migratory species in Macaronesia. It does so by examining whether these species recurrently
L10270: use multiple archipelagos and/or transit waters, such that key risks and management responsibilities
L10271: extend beyond individual archipelagos.
L10272: 
L10273: C_01 is reported as a structured, evidence-based connectivity profile, rather than a single numeric
L10274: index, enabling transparent ex-ante reasoning on corridor relevance and scenario implications.
L10275: 
L10276: The indicator describes corridor-relevant ecological connectivity using species occurrence and sight-
L10277: ings patterns for a DA-validated shortlist of highly migratory and/or vulnerable taxa (cetaceans, sea-
L10278: birds, elasmobranchs). It captures three connectivity signals:
L10279: 
L10280:     1. Cross-area occurrence: whether priority species are recorded in more than one archipelago
L10281:          and/or in Transit/ABNJ waters.
L10282: 
L10283:     2. Recurrence and seasonality: repeated, multi-year use and seasonal timing within archipelagos
L10284:          and in transit waters.
L10285: 
L10286:     3. Stepping-stone plausibility (contextual interpretation): whether detections align with known
L10287:          movement ecology and corridor-relevant features (e.g. slope/seamount use by deep divers;
L10288:          broad pelagic foraging ranges for seabirds).
L10289: 
L10290: C_01 provides a connectivity proxy based on recurrence and cross-area overlap. It does not estimate
L10291: abundance, density, or population trends.
L10292: 
L10293: Used in the corridor assessment to
L10294: 
L10295:     • Assess whether corridor-scale coordination adds value beyond archipelago-by-archipelago
L10296:          management for priority migratory taxa, where seasonal use and movement pathways span
L10297:          multiple jurisdictions (including transit waters).
L10298: 
L10299:     • Identify which parts of the corridor matter for which taxa: distinguishing species largely as-
L10300:          sociated with archipelago waters from those that also show a transit-water signal, so corridor
L10301:          measures can be focused on the relevant space and season (rather than applying generic cor-
L10302:          ridor claims everywhere).
L10303: 
L10304:     • Translate corridor scenarios into expected changes in “connectivity conditions”: whether
L10305:          pressures relevant to mobile species (e.g., bycatch risk, disturbance) are addressed more con-
L10306:          tinuously along movement routes across archipelagos and—under S4—across transit waters,
L10307:          without implying that animals move more or populations increase.
L10308: 
L10309:     • Support corridor design choices that are specific and testable: specifying where and when
L10310:          coherence matters most (e.g., seasons of recurrent use; areas of repeated occurrence) so pro-
L10311:          visions can be stated clearly and later evaluated.
L10312: 
L10313:     • Provide ecological grounding for cross-WP synthesis and scenario reasoning: clarifying which
L10314:          taxa, where, and when corridor coherence is most relevant when linking connectivity to
L10315: 
L10316:                                                                                                                        248
L10317:          pressures, habitats, and governance—while keeping conclusions proportionate to an ex-ante
L10318:          assessment.
L10319: 
L10320: Why this is relevant for a Macaronesia ecological corridor: governance touchpoints and sector sen-
L10321: sitivity
L10322: 
L10323: Macaronesia’s corridor logic rests on the fact that migratory species and mobile pressures do not align
L10324: with archipelago-by-archipelago management. C_01 is relevant because it links cross-boundary move-
L10325: ment patterns to concrete governance and sector touchpoints:
L10326: 
L10327:     • Multi-jurisdiction governance: species use spans Azores–Madeira–Canaries and offshore wa-
L10328:          ters, while management responsibility is fragmented across regional authorities, Member
L10329:          States, and ABNJ frameworks; C_01 provides a shared ecological rationale for coordinated
L10330:          measures and aligned monitoring expectations.
L10331: 
L10332:     • Fisheries interaction and bycatch sensitivity: many corridor-relevant taxa (seabirds, sharks,
L10333:          some cetaceans) are sensitive to bycatch and gear interactions; C_01 helps target where co-
L10334:          herence in mitigation is most consequential along movement pathways.
L10335: 
L10336:     • Shipping and operational disturbance: transit waters intersect with shipping routes (and as-
L10337:          sociated noise/collision risk); CETUS-derived transit signals help identify where corridor-wide
L10338:          risk management coordination is most relevant.
L10339: 
L10340:     • Tourism and whale-watching: near-island recurrence (e.g., Azores) is economically and so-
L10341:          cially salient; C_01 grounds corridor narratives in where and when flagship taxa are recurrent,
L10342:          supporting balanced corridor communication and acceptance.
L10343: 
L10344:     • Claimability of corridor benefits (link to C_02): C_01 clarifies what corridor relevance is being
L10345:          claimed for mobile taxa (where/when/which taxa); C_02 assesses whether monitoring and co-
L10346:          ordination capacity is sufficient to credibly support those claims.
L10347: 
L10348: Core interpretive statement
L10349: 
L10350: C_01 indicates whether priority migratory species show recurrent, seasonal use of multiple archipela-
L10351: gos and/or transit waters, providing an applied, evidence-based proxy for ecological connectivity. A
L10352: stronger C_01 signal (multi-year recurrence plus cross-area overlap) supports the case that a Macaro-
L10353: nesia corridor can deliver ecological value through more continuous, cross-jurisdiction management
L10354: of mobile species and mobile pressures. Where signals are weaker or uneven, C_01 indicates that cor-
L10355: ridor relevance should be framed more cautiously for those taxa/areas within the assembled evidence
L10356: base, recognising detectability and coverage limitations.

L10427: 
L10428: Baseline evidence uses taxon-appropriate primary datasets, selected to maximise coverage and inter-
L10429: pretability for connectivity while keeping the indicator proportionate.
L10430: 
L10431: Cetaceans
L10432: 
L10433:     • MONICET (Azores; 2009–2020): whale-watching sightings used to characterise near-island re-
L10434:          currence and seasonality in the Azores.
L10435: 
L10436:     • CETUS (since 2012): effort-based visual surveys by trained observers onboard cargo ships
L10437:          along repeated routes in the Eastern North Atlantic; used as the primary source for transit-

L10440: Seabirds
L10441: 
L10442:     • eBird / GBIF-derived occurrence records: primary source for archipelago-level presence, re-
L10443:          currence, and seasonality (e.g., breeding vs migration periods). Records are restricted to

L10446: Elasmobranchs
L10447: 
L10448:     • OBIS/GBIF georeferenced occurrences (and analogous curated occurrence datasets where
L10449:          available) are used as the primary baseline for presence/recurrence and cross-area overlap.

L10451:          with fishing activity, catchability, or exploitation dynamics. Occurrence evidence is interpreted
L10452:          conservatively as corridor-relevant use, not abundance.

L10455: 
L10456:     • Citizen-science platforms (e.g., iNaturalist): used selectively to confirm presence or seasonal
L10457:          signals; treated as opportunistic evidence.
L10458: 
L10459:     • Regional strandings and sightings networks: used to validate presence, capture episodic
L10460:          events, and triangulate seasonality; strandings are interpreted cautiously and not as proxies
L10461:          for live at-sea distribution.

L10489: Key assumptions and limitations
L10490: 
L10491: Interpretation scope
L10492:     • The baseline is a connectivity proxy: repeated use (recurrence), seasonal overlap, and cross-
L10493:          area occurrence.
L10494:     • Record counts are not abundance and do not indicate density or trends without effort stand-
L10495:          ardisation and bias correction.
L10496: 
L10497: Main limitations
L10498:     • Bounding boxes are a simplification: they include some non-island waters and exclude others;
L10499:          EEZ/polygon buffers would be cleaner if GIS layers become available.
L10500:     • CETUS is route-biased: detections reflect repeated cargo routes rather than uniform coverage
L10501:          of corridor waters (though effort-based design improves interpretability relative to ad hoc
L10502:          sightings).
L10503:     • MONICET is opportunistic: whale-watching effort varies with tourism patterns and weather;
L10504:          detection probability is not constant across space/time.
L10505:     • eBird/GBIF and citizen-science data are heterogeneous: strong nearshore/accessibility and
L10506:          observer-effort biases; these datasets support presence/seasonality but require conservative
L10507:          interpretation for offshore/transit waters.
L10508:     • Stranding data are confirmatory, not distributional: strandings validate occurrence but are
L10509:          influenced by drift, reporting probability, and carcass persistence.
L10510:     • Elasmobranch occurrence records are often sparse offshore: absence of records should not
L10511:          be interpreted as ecological absence; evidence strength explicitly reflects data sparsity and
L10512:          sampling bias, and resulting connectivity conclusions are framed as indicative.

L10686: Step 2 — Scenario response logic:
L10687: Objective:
L10688: Translate corridor scenarios (S1–S4) into bounded, ex-ante expectations for how connectivity-relevant
L10689: conditions for the priority migratory taxa are likely to change relative to the Step 1 baseline (recur-
L10690: rence, seasonality, cross-area overlap; archipelago vs transit signals). The purpose is not to predict
L10691: distribution shifts or recovery, but to assess whether corridor governance would make the movement
L10692: pathway less fragmented and the corridor claim more defensible.
L10693: 
L10694: Corridor-relevant pathways (how scenarios translate into fisheries effects)
L10695: 
L10696: C_01 is sensitive to three applied pathways that can change within an ex-ante horizon:
L10697: 
L10698:     1. Continuity of risk management along movement pathways (continuity pathway)
L10699:          Whether pressures that matter for mobile taxa are addressed consistently across archipelagos
L10700:          and, under S4, across transit waters (e.g., coherent seasonal windows, harmonised mitigation
L10701:          expectations, consistent disturbance management).
L10702: 
L10703:     2. Governance discontinuities and displacement of pressures (gap pathway)
L10704:          Whether mismatched rules create “gaps” along a movement pathway—so pressures are re-
L10705:          duced in one area/season but effectively persist or intensify in the adjacent link (between ar-
L10706:          chipelagos or between EEZ and ABNJ). This is about pressure displacement, not animals “leak-
L10707:          ing.”
L10708: 
L10709:     3. Claimability of connectivity (evidence pathway)
L10710:          Whether the corridor claim becomes easier to sustain because monitoring expectations, defi-
L10711:          nitions, and reporting become more comparable (S2) and coverage extends into transit wa-
L10712:          ters (S4). This affects how robustly Step 1 patterns can be tracked over time.
L10713: 
L10714: Timing logic (what moves first)
L10715: 
L10716: • Near-term (ex-ante horizon): change is most plausible in coherence of measures
L10717:     (where/when/what is managed) and claimability (shared framing, comparability of evi-
L10718:     dence), not in the animals’ use patterns.
L10719: 
L10720: • Medium-term: if coherence is sustained and pressures are reduced along the pathway,
L10721:     distribution/use signals may become more stable; this would be corroboration, not a pri-
L10722:     mary expected outcome.

L11106: Step 1 —Baseline construction (current state)
L11107: Objective:
L11108: Establish the baseline level of tuna dependency in each archipelago’s local commercial landings and
L11109: landing system, as the reference point for assessing exposure and adjustment pressure under different
L11110: corridor scenarios.
L11111: Method (baseline quantities):
L11112: For each archipelago, the baseline is calculated using a 3-year rolling average (2022–2024) to smooth
L11113: interannual variability:
L11114: 
L11115:     • Total commercial landings (tons),
L11116:     • Tuna landings (tons) aggregated across the selected highly migratory tuna species33, and
L11117:     • F_02 baseline = tuna landings / total landings (ratio of means)
L11118: 
L11119: Interpretation:
L11120:     • A higher baseline F_02 indicates that local fleets and the onshore landing/value-chain system
L11121:          (ports, first-sale channels, processors) are currently more structurally reliant on tuna, and
L11122:          therefore more exposed/sensitive to tuna-relevant governance change (e.g., monitoring and
L11123:          enforcement intensity, access conditions, spatial/temporal rules).
L11124: 
L11125:     • A lower baseline F_02 indicates lower direct exposure and potentially greater buffering capac-
L11126:          ity within the fisheries sector.
L11127: 
L11128: Why this matters for the corridor:
L11129: The corridor is expected to primarily change how tuna fisheries are governed, monitored, and coordi-
L11130: nated across space, rather than to prescribe/mandate higher or lower catches. Establishing baseline
L11131: tuna dependency therefore identifies where corridor-related governance change will be felt first in
L11132: operational and economic terms, and where adjustment pressures and distributional effects are most
L11133: likely to concentrate.
L11134: Baseline caveat (what the baseline represents):
L11135: F_02 reflects the composition of recorded local landings. It is therefore sensitive to reporting practices
L11136: and where fish are landed (e.g., landings may shift between ports/jurisdictions under different control
L11137: regimes). This is consistent with the indicator’s purpose—capturing exposure of the local landing sys-
L11138: tem—but it means F_02 should not be read as a measure of stock status or ecosystem performance.

L11164: Step 2 — Scenario response logic: from governance change to fisheries effects
L11165: Objective:
L11166: Translate corridor scenarios (S1–S4) into plausible, bounded changes in recorded local landings
L11167: through governance and behavioural pathways, not biological production. Any scenario landings
L11168: changes are represented as bounded, governance-driven deviations from the baseline that plausibly
L11169: arise from effort allocation, compliance/traceability, enforcement coherence, and reporting/landing-
L11170: location choices.
L11171: Core assumption
L11172: The corridor is treated as an institutional and operational intervention. In practice, measures may
L11173: include:
L11174: 
L11175:     • spatial/seasonal access rules,
L11176: 
L11177:     • gear-related regulations (including FAD-related measures where relevant),
L11178: 
L11179:     • strengthened monitoring and control (e-monitoring/observers, AIS/VMS analytics, port con-
L11180:          trols),
L11181: 
L11182:     • improved coordination across EEZs and adjacent waters (including enforcement alignment).
L11183: 
L11184: These measures are expected to influence fisheries outcomes primarily by changing behaviour and
L11185: governance conditions (where/when/how fishing occurs; compliance and documentation), rather
L11186: than by directly increasing allowable catch or “creating fish.”
L11187: Results chain used for scenario logic
L11188: 
L11189:     1. Intervention layer: corridor rules, monitoring intensity, enforcement coordination.
L11190: 
L11191:     2. Human response: changes in location/timing, targeting, gear choice, compliance behaviour,
L11192:          and landing decisions (where fish are brought to port).
L11193: 
L11194:     3. Pressure / control signal: changes in effective fishing pressure and the share of catch that is
L11195:          legal, documented and reported.
L11196: 
L11197:     4. Outcomes (near-term focus): changes in the composition and stability of recorded local land-
L11198:          ings (tuna vs non-tuna), and spillovers into substitute fisheries.
L11199:          (Longer-term biological responses and TAC/allocation changes are treated as sensitivity con-
L11200:          siderations only, given time lags and institutional constraints.)
L11201: 
L11202: Near-term vs longer-term effects
L11203: In the near term, tuna catches and landings are bounded by management and allocation constraints
L11204: (e.g., ICCAT TACs/access arrangements) and by operational realities. Therefore, scenarios are not as-
L11205: sumed to generate sustained increases in tuna production. Instead, corridor scenarios can produce
L11206: measurable signals through:
L11207: 
L11208:                                                                                                                        266
L11209:     • Where/when effects (effort distribution): spatial/temporal rules and avoidance responses
L11210:          under higher control change effort allocation and targeting patterns.
L11211: 
L11212:     • How effects (control/compliance/recording): monitoring and enforcement coherence af-
L11213:          fects the share of catch that is compliant, documented and recorded, and can shift where
L11214:          landings occur.
L11215: 
L11216:     • Spillover effects (displacement/substitution): changes in tuna governance conditions can
L11217:          reallocate effort toward non-tuna fisheries (pelagic and potentially demersal), with distribu-
L11218:          tional and management implications.
L11219: 
L11220: Operationalisation: translating scenario narratives into bounded deviations
L11221: Scenarios are evaluated ex-ante by applying bounded multipliers to baseline quantities:
L11222: 
L11223:     • Tuna landings (recorded locally)
L11224: 
L11225:     • Non-tuna landings (recorded locally)
L11226: 
L11227:     • (Optional) tuna value €/t — only under an explicit activation rule (see below)
L11228: 
L11229: These multipliers represent plausible deviations in recorded local landings caused by governance/be-
L11230: haviour/reporting effects (including landing-location changes), not predicted changes in stock abun-
L11231: dance. Scenario effects are therefore expressed as ranges (and where useful, expected changes in
L11232: volatility), not point forecasts.
L11233: Accounting identities
L11234: 
L11235:     • Non-tuna (baseline) = Total − Tuna
L11236: 
L11237:     • Total (scenario) = Tuna (scenario) + Non-tuna (scenario)
L11238: 
L11239:     • F_02 (scenario) = Tuna (scenario) / Total (scenario) × 100
L11240: 
L11241: Use of historical record to constrain assumptions
L11242: Long-term fisheries data (e.g., 2007–2025 for the Canaries) are used to define plausibility bands for
L11243: tuna dependency (e.g., min–max, 10th–90th percentile range, CV). Scenario multipliers are con-
L11244: strained to:
L11245: 
L11246:     • remain within observed historical ranges, or
L11247: 
L11248:     • be explicitly justified as outside-range due to a clearly specified governance mechanism.
L11249: 
L11250: This makes assumptions empirically anchored and consistent across archipelagos.

L11253: where do we expect economic sensitivity, redistribution of effort, and adjustment pressures under
L11254: different corridor scenarios:
L11255: 
L11256:     • A decline in F_02 can reflect (i) reduced local tuna landings due to tighter access/control and
L11257:          higher compliance burden/costs, (ii) substitution into non-tuna fisheries, and/or (iii) changes
L11258:          in recording/landing location under improved monitoring (e.g. less under-/misreporting).
L11259: 
L11260:     • An increase in F_02 can occur without tuna growth (e.g., non-tuna declines, or reporting/land-
L11261:          ing patterns shift) and should be treated as an exposure signal rather than an ecological out-
L11262:          come.

L11767: Objective:

L11769: criteria (EE, EC, EQ, PO, FI), using a bounded attribution logic that distinguishes (i) availability/pressure
L11770: signals from (ii) targeting/displacement/catchability artefacts. CPUE is treated as an EE-facing signal

L11772: ance evidence.

L11777:          Interprets ΔF_03 as a signal consistent with changing availability to the fishery under compa-
L11778:          rable fishing power and targeting, conditional on IQ and displacement context.

L11781:          Interprets ΔF_03 as a change in catch outcomes per input, informing likely shifts in unit
L11782:          costs, trip success, and operational efficiency (strongest when paired with effort and value
L11783:          proxies). FAO treats CPUE jointly with effort as informative for exploitation dynamics and op-
L11784:          erational signals.

L11844: F_03 is most informative for diagnosing whether the corridor is creating controllability and reducing
L11845: leakage (S4 > S3 > S2), but it will rarely provide a clean early signal of ecological recovery; early CPUE
L11846: “improvements” can still coincide with displacement or effort concentration.
L11847: Near-term vs longer-term:
L11848: 
L11849:     • Near-term (ex-ante horizon): In early years, CPUE changes are more likely dominated by be-
L11850:          haviour, access and compliance/leakage dynamics (e.g. redistribution/targeting/reporting)
L11851:          rather than biomass response or stock recovery
L11852:

L11856: Method
L11857: Decision rules (used consistently across scenarios)
L11858: EE-supporting inference (strongest form):
L11859: CPUE is interpreted as EE-consistent (positive) when CPUE stabilises/increases and the scenario im-
L11860: plies stable/reduced effort in corridor-relevant strata and leakage/displacement is bounded and mon-
L11861: itoring/compliance credibility strengthens.
L11862: EE-negative inference (strongest form):
L11863: CPUE decline with stable/increasing effort or persistent leakage/displacement is interpreted as EE-in-
L11864: consistent (negative).
L11865: Ambiguity conditions (explicitly flagged):
L11866: CPUE increases are treated as ambiguous when driven plausibly by effort concentration, targeting
L11867: shifts, or inconsistent reporting/catchability (hyperstability/effort-creep risks).
L11868: Each scenario assessment reports (i) EE direction and (ii) confidence (High/Medium/Low), where con-
L11869: fidence depends on segmentation/standardisation feasibility and the displacement/compliance con-
L11870: text.

L11873:

L11879: targeting and spatial concentration effects.

L11898: Indicator definition and purpose
L11899: F_04 indicates fisheries participation and capacity exposure that could be affected by corridor gov-
L11900: ernance in Macaronesia. It uses register- or licence-based counts by archipelago and segment:
L11901: 
L11902:     • F_04a Commercial fleet: commercial fishing vessels associated with the archipelago prefera-
L11903:          bly by home port / operational base
L11904: 
L11905:     • F_04b Fishing charter: licensed/authorised recreational fishing charter operators and/or ves-
L11906:          sel (preferred “business unit” measure, depending on what each administration publishes).
L11907: 
L11908: Charter fishing is treated here as a commercial service that provides recreational angling fishing expe-
L11909: riences/trips. Accordingly, charter operators/vessels are typically recorded under maritime-tourism or
L11910: recreational/enterprise licensing systems rather than commercial fishing fleet registers, and should
L11911: not be assumed to be included in commercial vessel counts.
L11912: F_04 therefore separates commercial from charter vessels, with an explicit rule34 to avoid double
L11913: counting.
L11914: 
L11915: Used in the corridor assessment to:
L11916: 
L11917:     1. Map stakeholder salience35 and distributional sensitivity (EQ/PO): where corridor govern-
L11918:          ance change is most likely to be socio-economically and politically “felt” first (scale of af-
L11919:          fected operators and visibility of burdens).
L11920: 
L11921:     2. Anticipate adjustment pressure (EC/FI supporting): where operators are more likely to face
L11922:          changes in operating conditions (access constraints, compliance burden) and respond
L11923:          through product switching, spatial re-targeting, season adjustments, and (longer-term) en-
L11924:          try/exit dynamics.
L11925: 
L11926:     3. Flag plausible substitution pathways between commercial and charter segments for tar-
L11927:          geted triangulation (not inferred from counts alone).

L11930: sitivity
L11931: 
L11932: An ecological corridor is expected to affect fisheries-related ocean use primarily through
L11933:     (i) access and spatial/seasonal rules,
L11934:     (ii) monitoring, reporting and control requirements, and
L11935:

L11937: corresponding to the primary operating mode (or exclude from one segment) to avoid inflating totals.
L11938: 35 Stakeholder salience = how visible and politically important the issue/actor becomes once rules change (i.e.,
L11939: who notices first, who mobilises, and who can block or enable implementation).

L11942:     (iii) perceived fairness and enforceability across jurisdictions (including “leakage” and per-
L11943:               ceived competitive disadvantages).
L11944: 
L11945: In Macaronesia, commercial fishing and charter-based recreational fishing are governed through dis-
L11946: tinct licensing and reporting systems, which creates clear administrative “touchpoints” for corridor
L11947: implementation and makes participation/capacity counts a practical way to map where governance
L11948: change will be most visible, contested, and economically consequential.
L11949: 
L11950: The commercial fishing fleet is the primary “exposure group” for corridor governance measures (zon-
L11951: ing, access constraints, licensing/enforcement), because it reflects how many operators and vessels
L11952: may face displacement, compliance costs, or opportunity changes. The scale is non-trivial across Mac-
L11953: aronesia (see Table 1). Fleet structure also implies high sensitivity to spatial rules: in the Azores, ~73%
L11954: of the active fleet is <10 m and hooks-and-lines dominate (~84%), limiting flexibility to “move else-
L11955: where” when access changes. In the Canaries, a large share of the fleet is similarly “small-scale-gear”
L11956: oriented. Commercial fisheries are also a livelihood/value pillar (e.g., Azores bottom fisheries are de-
L11957: scribed as the main fishery by landed value, representing ~75% of landed value on average), so even
L11958: modest governance-driven shifts can have meaningful welfare and political-implementability implica-
L11959: tions.
L11960: 
L11961: Charter fishing is also very governance-sensitive because it sits at the tourism–fisheries interface: rule
L11962: changes can translate quickly into lost flexibility, cancellations, crowding, compliance time/cost, and
L11963: product switching, even if licence counts themselves are slow-moving.
L11964: Macaronesia-specific anchors (where this shows up in practice):
L11965: 
L11966:      • Azores (PT): Charter activity is formally organised under maritime-tourism modalities, including “pesca
L11967:           turística” (Regional Directorate for Maritime Policies), and is publicly reflected through the licensed op-
L11968:           erator listings, making operator counts the most defensible baseline unit, with vessel authorisations as
L11969:           a capacity qualifier
L11970: 
L11971:      • Madeira (PT): The charter segment has material welfare relevance: a peer-reviewed study (Pita et al.
L11972:           2022)36 estimates annual charter-boat recreation value at €6.3M (range €5.2–7.5M), comparable to the
L11973:           reported annual economic impact of Madeira’s commercial fishing sector (~€12M), and identifies 14
L11974:           charter companies operating 18 vessels (benchmarking against 7 companies with 10 boats in Galicia).
L11975:           This makes corridor design choices that affect nearshore access or compliance burdens politically and
L11976:           distributionally salient, not “just tourism.”
L11977: 
L11978:      • Canary Islands (ES): Charter fishing (e.g. big-game and sport fishing) is visible and highly popular through
L11979:           vessel licensing requirements and renewal cycles; one widely cited baseline indicates ~90 registered
L11980:           recreational fishing charter vessels,37 with licences renewed every three years, providing a concrete
L11981:           governance lever for corridor-related compliance and reporting expectations. In a tourism-dependent
L11982:           regional economy (trade/transport/hospitality/leisure accounting for a large GDP share), charter con-
L11983:           straints can quickly become politically salient.

L11988: appear first in operating conditions and active participation (trip-days, cancellations, compliance fric-
L11989: tion, crowding, switching), while register/licence counts mainly indicate stake and longer-run adjust-

L11996: Core interpretive statement
L11997: Adjustment Pressure and Trends: A change in the count of licensed commercial fishers and charters
L11998: over time can reveal how much “adjustment pressure” governance measures are exerting on the sec-
L11999: tor.
L12000: A higher F_04 baseline indicates greater socio-economic stake and governance sensitivity in fisher-
L12001: ies-related ocean use: more operators/vessels are potentially affected by corridor rules, and the like-
L12002: lihood increases that implementation triggers adaptation, contestation, and distributional impacts.
L12003: The segment mix matters: commercial fleet counts mainly indicate exposure to monitoring/access re-
L12004: gimes and sectoral adjustment pressures; charter capacity mainly indicates sensitivity at the tourism–
L12005: fisheries interface, where operational flexibility and compliance burdens can quickly translate into ac-
L12006: ceptability risks for small businesses. For example, a dramatic decline in charter operator numbers
L12007: following new regulations usually signifies that the policy pressure is high – perhaps too high for some
L12008: businesses to cope.
L12009: Importantly, a high F_04 does not imply high fishing pressure; it implies high governance salience and
L12010: potential distributional sensitivity.
L12011: 
L12012: What F04 does not do (guardrails)
L12013: F_04 is not a direct measure of fishing effort, catch, stock status, or biodiversity outcomes.
L12014: Licence and register counts are sticky and shaped by tourism cycles, fuel prices, macroeconomic con-
L12015: ditions, and administrative practices. In the near term, corridor effects are expected to appear first as
L12016: changes in active participation and operating conditions (trip-days/season length, cancellations,
L12017: crowding in open areas, compliance time/cost), with entry/exit likely reflected in counts only long-
L12018: term.
L12019: For this reason, F_04 is interpreted as an indicator of exposure, friction, and distributional impacts.
L12020: Any inference about displacement or environmental implications is conditional and must be triangu-
L12021: lated with spatial pressure indicators, enforcement capacity, and segment-specific effort/catch proxies
L12022: (where available).

L12159: Step 2 — Scenario response logic: from governance change to fisheries effects
L12160: 
L12161: Objective:
L12162: Translate corridor scenarios (S1–S4) into bounded, ex-ante expectations for how fisheries participa-
L12163: tion and operating conditions are likely to change across (a) commercial fleet exposure (F_04a) and
L12164: (b) charter capacity and activity (F_04b) in Macaronesia. The purpose is not to predict fleet size, but
L12165: to anticipate where adjustment pressures and implementation frictions concentrate.
L12166: 
L12167: Corridor-relevant pathways (how scenarios translate into fisheries effects)
L12168: 
L12169: In Macaronesia, corridor governance most plausibly affects participation and operating conditions
L12170: through four linked pathways that apply to both segments, with different “first-order” manifestations:
L12171: 
L12172: 1. Access and spatial/seasonal rules (constraint + crowding pathway)
L12173:     • Commercial: constraints on where/when fishing is feasible can shift effort to substitute
L12174:          grounds/species and increase crowding/competition.
L12175:     • Charter: place-based trip planning means even small access changes translate quickly into
L12176:          lost flexibility, cancellations, and crowding in remaining accessible sites (high nearshore visi-
L12177:          bility).
L12178: 
L12179: 2. Monitoring, reporting and control requirements (compliance-friction pathway)
L12180:     • Commercial: higher monitoring/reporting and inspections raise fixed and variable operating
L12181:          costs (time, equipment, admin).
L12182:     • Charter: compliance burdens tend to be more regressive (small operators), and licens-
L12183:          ing/tourism interfaces make rule clarity and administrative simplicity decisive for viability.
L12184: 
L12185: 3. Displacement and substitution (spillover pathway)
L12186:     • Commercial: effort displaced from corridor-sensitive areas can re-target to other fisheries/ar-
L12187:          eas, shifting pressure and potentially creating conflict.
L12188:     • Charter: substitution is often product switching (e.g., from fishing trips to sightseeing/whale
L12189:          watching) or spatial switching, rather than exit.
L12190: 
L12191: 4. Credibility and fairness narratives (legitimacy pathway)
L12192:     • Commercial: perceived “escape routes” (leakage) undermine acceptance and increase contes-
L12193:          tation, even if ecological intent is strong.
L12194: 
L12195:                                                                                                                        288
L12196: • Charter: public-facing cancellations and “unfair burdens” are politically salient; legitimacy de-
L12197:     pends on whether benefits are visible locally and whether compliance demands are propor-
L12198:     tionate.
L12199: 
L12200: Key implication: Stronger governance intensity (S3/S4) increases both the potential for stabilisa-
L12201: tion/credibility and the risk of friction and uneven burdens. S2 primarily affects clarity and predicta-
L12202: bility rather than access.
L12203: 
L12204: Timing logic (what moves first)
L12205: 
L12206: • Near-term (ex-ante horizon): effects appear first in activity and operating conditions, not
L12207:     in register counts (trip-days, cancellations, crowding, compliance time/cost, gear/area
L12208:     switching).
L12209: 
L12210: • Medium-term: sustained regimes can shift entry/exit and business models (consolida-
L12211:     tion, diversification) if margins change materially.
